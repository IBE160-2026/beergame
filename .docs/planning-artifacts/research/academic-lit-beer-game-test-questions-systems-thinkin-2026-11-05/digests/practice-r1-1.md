## Findings

### A. Question formats actually used

1. claim: The strongest published "test item" for understanding delay + stock management is a graph-sketching task, not a beer-game item per se: the Manufacturing Case (MC). A make-to-stock firm, customer orders step from 10,000 to 11,000 widgets/week in week 5, 4-week production lag, firm wants constant desired inventory of 50,000. Subjects sketch production (and in one condition inventory) over time. Correct answer has no unique shape but must satisfy: inventory first falls (to 46,000 by week 9), production must overshoot orders, overshoot area equals the lost inventory (4,000 widgets = conservation of material), production and inventory graphs must be consistent. Scoring was by coded criteria (7 items: equilibrium start, lag, overshoot, area conservation, initial inventory decline, subsequent recovery, consistency of production vs inventory). source: S1; publisher: System Dynamics Society; pub_date: 2000; confidence: high; class: example-item + rubric.

2. claim: Bathtub / cash-flow (BT/CF) tasks: sketch the stock trajectory given a net-flow pattern (square wave task 1; sawtooth task 2), with two cover stories (bathtub vs company cash balance). Coded per criterion (stock rises when inflow > outflow, peaks at right times, slope equals net rate, continuity, etc.). Directly reusable as a "predict inventory/backlog from given order/shipment series" item. source: S1; same; confidence: high; class: example-item + rubric.

3. claim: A real course-exam example (take-home, 100%): Q1 Bullwhip (50%): (a) explain concept, give real-life example, discuss mitigation from business and consumer perspective, ~800 words essay, ChatGPT required and prompts must be listed; (b) "Compare this with your experience in playing the Beer Game earlier. What are the points that your experience playing the Beer Game can confirm?"; (c) a personal real-life experience explained by the bullwhip theory; (d) suggestions to improve the game. Q2 (50%): why instructors use simulation games, lecture vs game, "What have you learnt through playing the games." Format is long essay and own-experience reflection; no answer key, no individualised data from the student's own run. source: S4; publisher: CYCU Temple program (Taiwan); pub_date: 2024-04-18; confidence: medium (single course exam, only exam found); class: example-item.

4. claim: The McMahon controlled experiment tested knowledge with short test items labelled by theme: Q1 definitional (bullwhip definition), Q3 "Sources of Volatility" coded in sub-variables (information, consumer demand, no collaboration, delays, supply-chain structure; plus "consumer unrelated" distractor), Q4 "Volatility gap along supply chain" (how variance grows upstream). Item wording not in the slides. Q3 coded as set-of-causes with an unrelated-cause distractor is a usable format. source: S2; publisher: Univ. Warwick / Economics Network; pub_date: 2011-09-06; confidence: medium (slides only); class: example-item (structure only) + empirical-result.

5. claim: Debrief prompts used by instructors are experiential/structural, not graded: "Did you feel yourself controlled by forces in the system...? Or did you feel in control?"; "Did you find yourself 'blaming' the groups next to you?"; "What, if anything, is unrealistic about this game?"; "Why are there order delays?"; "Why have both distributor and wholesalers; why not ship beer directly from the factory to the retailer?". Core take-away: structure, not individuals, drives behaviour. Page gives no assessment guidance. Vendor guide (S7) adds: compare end-customer demand with orders at each echelon (bullwhip), when did costs peak, propose fixes; winner chosen by total inventory + backlog cost. These are good seeds for explain-the-mechanism and "what would you change" items but are discussion prompts, not validated test items. source: S3, S7; publisher: beergame.org / Zensimu; pub_date: undated; confidence: medium (S3) / low (S7); class: example-item.

### B. What separates real understanding from guessing / memorising

6. claim (key finding): Having played the beer game raised MC-task scores (46% vs 33% mean, t=3.35, p<0.001) and made oscillating production sketches more likely (48% vs 35%; r=0.24), BUT the gain was an artifact of recalling "wild oscillation". Beer-game players got credit for production overshoot (53% vs 30%) and initial inventory decline (77% vs 55%) and recovery (66% vs 43%) only because any oscillation implies overshoot; there was NO significant difference on conservation-of-material (area), or on consistency of production vs inventory graphs, and no effect on the BT/CF tasks. One subject wrote in the margin "Some sort of wild variation (this is the beer game, right?)". Interpretation of the authors: players recalled the game's behaviour pattern without grasping stock-flow principles. Implication for test design: items must check area/conservation and graph-to-graph consistency, and must be novel in cover story, otherwise memorised patterns score. source: S1; 2000; confidence: high (peer-reviewed lineage, N~150, but self-selected beer-game exposure, MIT Sloan students, prior game not controlled); class: empirical-result.

7. claim: Baseline weakness is general: avg 77% on simplest square-wave task, 48% on sawtooth, 41% on MC; 89% of subjects drew production paths violating conservation of material; 89% drew inventory inconsistent with their own production path; only 44% showed overshoot. >half of subjects had already played the beer game. So correct answers on "does inventory rise when inflow > outflow" are weak discriminators; inconsistency checks and quantitative area reasoning discriminate better. source: S1; 2000; confidence: high; class: empirical-result.

8. claim: Asking for the inventory graph in addition to production did not help (63% vs 23% showing overshoot in the no-inventory-graph vs inventory-graph condition) - extra required output lowers performance under time pressure or exposes stock-flow misunderstanding; the authors could not separate the two explanations. Practical: time-box and keep each sketch item single-purpose. source: S1; 2000; confidence: medium; class: empirical-result.

9. claim: McMahon found the game drove home the central idea but "may wrongly downplay related ideas", and "facts mastery or definitional aspects are better through slides"; effects strongest for better students; short-term gains on definitional item (Q1) for game group were positive after class but reversed/vanished at the exam 6 weeks later (Exam x Game coefficient -0.51 to -0.89 on Q1). Game group improved on "information" and "no collaboration" causes of volatility at the exam (q3info +0.61, q3nocollab +0.79) but not on delays or supply-chain structure causes (n.s.). So a test on the five Lee-Padmanabhan-Whang causes should not expect the game alone to carry batching/price/rationing. source: S2; 2011; confidence: medium (slides, coefficients read from text dump, ~130 students with panel data, self-selection into groups); class: empirical-result.

### C. Assessing the student's own decisions

10. claim: The anchor-and-adjust ordering rule (Sterman 1989) is the standard model of player behaviour; later work still uses it as the representation of human players, with decision parameters (stock adjustment fraction, weight of supply line) estimated from participants' orders and average estimates from Sterman's participants used as the "semi-rational" benchmark. Search summary (not verified in a read source) states typical estimates: adjustment fraction about 0.3, supply-line weight near zero (= supply-line neglect). I did not retrieve Sterman 1989 itself or any classroom use of per-student parameter estimation. source: S5 (rule and use of Sterman estimates, read); numbers from search snippet only; publisher: ISDC; pub_date: 2015; confidence: medium for the rule, low for the numeric values; class: empirical-result.

11. claim: No source found showing an instructor-validated test item built from the student's own order log (e.g. "week 9 you ordered 20 while demand was 8"). The closest are reflection prompts (S4 1b: compare your experience) and the vendor suggestion to compare demand vs orders per echelon (S7). Individualised items are therefore novel relative to the literature found; their validity is untested here. confidence: medium (absence after limited search); class: gap.

### D. Learning gain pre/post

12. claim: Thai logistics course, N=65, quasi-experimental one-group pre/post plus reflection forms, teamwork rubric, game record sheets, satisfaction questionnaire. Test content validity IOC 0.67-1.00, Cronbach alpha reported 0.91 (instrument reliability, exact scale unclear in garbled text). Mean score rose from 56.20 (SD 12.40) to 78.50 (SD 10.10), +22.3 points overall; sub-dimension gains of +19.5 to +25 (system variables/delays/goods in transit, bullwhip). Students rated the debrief and dashboard data most useful. Weaknesses: no control group, probable same-form pre/post, local Thai-language journal, test items not recoverable. source: S6; publisher: Journal of MCU Social Development (Mahachulalongkornrajavidyalaya Univ.); pub_date: 2026-06; confidence: medium-low (peer-reviewed regional journal, extraction partial); class: learning-gain.

13. claim (snippet only): IAFOR ACE2023 paper, 42 first-year logistics students, same knowledge test before and after, 95.12% scored higher on post-test; motivation by satisfaction survey. Not read. source: S8; confidence: low; class: learning-gain.

14. claim: Controlled design with delayed retention exists (McMahon: pre-test, post-test, mid-term 6 weeks later; game vs lecture vs none; diff-in-diff, ~200 students). It is the best-designed pre/post found, but is unpublished slides; the main result is that the game does NOT beat slides on definitions and its gains partly fade. source: S2; confidence: medium; class: learning-gain.

## Implications for the test (my reading, flagged as inference)
- Include at least one novel-cover-story graph item (inventory/pipeline after a demand step, with a built-in conservation check) rather than recalling "the game oscillated"; grade on area/consistency, not on oscillation (S1).
- Use MC for definitions and cause identification (S2: game is weaker on definitional recall) but add distractors that are unrelated causes (S2 Q3 format).
- Long answers: ask "explain from structure, not from people" (S3) and score on whether delay + feedback + ordering rule are named, not on blame language.
- Own-game items are unprecedented in what I found; treat as design bet.
