# Digest canon-r1-1: assessing understanding of stocks/flows, delays, feedback (beer-game mechanism)

Accessed 2026-11-05 for all. Budget used: about 15 tool calls. Only ONE primary paper was read in full text (Booth Sweeney and Sterman 2000, conference version). Everything else is from search-result summaries, landing pages or abstracts, and is marked as such. Several PDFs failed (403, 429, binary, bot-wall).

## Findings

F1. Instrument exists: "Bathtub Dynamics: Initial Results of a Systems Thinking Inventory" (Booth Sweeney and Sterman), System Dynamics Review 16(3), 2000, pp. 249-294. Brief tasks for stocks and flows, time delays and negative feedback. Pre-/post-test use was the stated goal. Not a validated scale. The authors call it "initial" and "a larger project to develop an instrument".
- source: https://mitmgmtfaculty.mit.edu/jsterman/bathtubdynamics/ (Sterman's page with abstract and citation; the journal citation also appears in a search summary). Publisher: MIT Sloan / J. Sterman faculty page. pub 2000. confidence high. class: instrument.

F2. Task formats, all read in full from the May 2000 conference version "Preliminary Results of a Systems Thinking Inventory" (Version 1.0, ICSD 2000 Bergen; peer-reviewed conference proceedings; later journal version superseded it; numbers may differ slightly).
- source: https://proceedings.systemdynamics.org/2000/PDFs/sweeney4.pdf (System Dynamics Society, 2000). confidence high for the conference version. class: instrument, example-item.
- Format throughout: a few paragraphs of text describing a situation. The respondent draws a graph of the expected behavior over time. High-school arithmetic only. About 10 minutes per session. Unpaid and ungraded. Scored with a coded rubric of 5-8 criteria per task.
- Task BT/CF 1 (square wave): a graph gives inflow and outflow rates. Both are piecewise constant; the example has inflow 75 then 50 l/s and constant outflow 50. Initial stock 100. The respondent sketches the stock's path. The cover story is either a bathtub (seconds) or a firm's bank account, with cash deposits and withdrawals (weeks). Rubric: stock rises when inflow > outflow; stock falls when outflow > inflow; no discontinuous jumps; peaks and troughs where net flow crosses zero; linear slope of +/-25 per period; quantity added per segment = 100, so peak 200 and trough 100.
- Task BT/CF 2 (sawtooth inflow, constant outflow): the same, but the inflow is non-constant. Rubric: slope of stock = net rate; stock rises at a diminishing rate while net flow is positive and falling, and so on (four cases); max slope 50/period; area of the triangle = 50 units, so the stock peaks at 150 and troughs at 50.
- Manufacturing Case (MC): a make-to-stock firm with a desired inventory of 50,000 widgets and a 4-week production delay. Orders step from 10,000 to 11,000 widgets/week at week 5. The respondent sketches the trajectory of production (and, in one condition, inventory). Rubric: starts in equilibrium; production lags by 4 weeks; production OVERSHOOTS orders to rebuild inventory; the overshoot area equals the inventory lost (4,000 units, conservation of material); inventory first falls (production < orders), then recovers; inventory trajectory consistent with production and orders. Oscillation was coded but did not score. There was a "feedback hint" treatment, with and without the phrase "they adjust production schedules to close any gap between the desired and actual level".
- There is also a "paper fold" task (exponential growth, positive feedback), mentioned but not reported there.

F3. Performance. Subjects were MIT Sloan students (MBA and others, more than half with engineering, science or maths backgrounds; n about 143-225 per task). Results below are from the full text, conference version:
- source: https://proceedings.systemdynamics.org/2000/PDFs/sweeney4.pdf. confidence high (conference version). class: empirical-result.
- BT/CF Task 1 (easiest): about 80% correctly show the stock rising/falling according to net flow; 86% place peaks and troughs correctly; only 63% relate net rate to change in stock over an interval; 78% relate net rate to slope; 11% draw discontinuous jumps in the stock (the stock traces the shape of the flow, "matching" pattern). Bathtub did better than cash flow (t=2.94, p<0.004). Some respondents show "spreadsheet thinking" (step function per week), mostly in the cash-flow version.
- BT/CF Task 2 (sawtooth): average 48%. Fewer than half show the stock rising/falling correctly. Only 40% put peaks and troughs correctly. 37% relate net rate to change in stock. 28% relate net rate to slope. About 5% draw "frequency-doubled" oscillation. A PhD physicist wrote the correct equations (F = dQ/dt, Q = integral of F) and then drew an unrelated curve.
- Manufacturing Case: average 41%. About half do not show correct equilibrium start or the 4-week lag. Only 44% show production overshooting orders. 89% violate conservation of material (overshoot area does not equal the lost inventory), a figure the authors call "shocking". 68% show inventory initially declining; 56% show inventory recovering; 10% give an inventory trajectory consistent with production and orders. Most drew production adjusting to the new order rate with a lag but no overshoot.
- Effect of demographics: no consistent pattern for education, degree, age, gender or language (a few items significant: prior field on Task 1 p=0.006; region on Task 1). The abstract (Sterman page) states "performance did not vary systematically with prior education, age, national origin, or other demographic variables".

F4. Beer-game experience effect (directly relevant to the course). More than half the subjects had played the beer game earlier. Those who had played scored higher on the Manufacturing Case (46% vs 33%, t=3.35, p<0.001) and drew oscillation more often (48% vs 35%; r=0.24). The authors argue the gain is likely an artifact: beer-game players got credit for production overshoot (53% vs 30%) and inventory first falling then recovering (77% vs 55%; 66% vs 43%) simply by drawing "wild variation" (one wrote "this is the beer game, right?"). There was NO significant difference on conservation of material, or consistency between production and inventory trajectories, and no effect on the BT/CF tasks (Table 6: p=0.99 and p=0.42). Interpretation: playing the game gives recognition of the pattern, not demonstrated understanding of stock-flow structure. Authors flag two competing explanations (real learning vs remembering). Strong implication for test design: test conservation/accounting logic, not recall of oscillation.
- source: https://proceedings.systemdynamics.org/2000/PDFs/sweeney4.pdf. confidence high (conference version). class: empirical-result, validity-evidence (construct caveat).
- Secondary: a search summary says the journal version reports that "playing the beer distribution game improved performance on the Manufacturing task" (from the web-search snippet, not verified in the journal text). confidence low.

F5. Explanation of the failures: the "correlation heuristic" (pattern matching: assume a stock's behavior looks like its flow's behavior), from Cronin, Gonzalez and Sterman (2009), "Why Don't Well-Educated Adults Understand Accumulation? A Challenge to Researchers, Educators and Citizens", Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 108(1), 116-130. Peer-reviewed. Only the abstract was read: highly educated people often cannot infer the behavior of simple stock-flow systems; poor performance is not attributable to inability to read graphs, lack of context knowledge, motivation or cognitive capacity; "robust phenomenon that appears to be difficult to overcome". The task variants (department store, bank account, CO2 tasks) are NOT confirmed in text I read.
- source: https://www.mit.edu/~jsterman/Why_Adults_Accumulation.html (Sterman page, abstract) and https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v108y2009i1p116-130.html (listed in search). Publisher: Elsevier / MIT. pub 2009. confidence high for the claim, low for the item details. class: empirical-result.
- A 2025 follow-up statement (from a search summary, unverified) that SD courses improve understanding but a minority still reason correlationally. confidence low.

F6. Climate stock-flow task (CO2 stabilization). Described through a secondary paper (Guy, Kashima, Walker, O'Neill, Climatic Change; manuscript copy at University of Melbourne repository) summarizing Sterman and Sweeney (2007): respondents with high education were given IPCC emissions and removal data and had to sketch an emissions path that would stabilize atmospheric CO2; 70% sketched a path that maintained or increased current emissions, which is incompatible with stabilization (mass balance: outflow must equal inflow). Moxnes and Saysel (2009) asked respondents to give emission rates at decadal points; an air-mattress analogy reduced overestimation; a balloon analogy made it worse. This is hearsay via a secondary source; the original was not read.
- source: https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/2fa4cdf7-c56f-5fa5-9c21-43a06a2774b1/content (University of Melbourne repository, accepted manuscript, peer-reviewed journal). confidence medium (secondary reporting of primary results). class: empirical-result, example-item.

F7. Beer game / feedback misperception, the foundational empirical papers: Sterman (1989), "Modeling Managerial Behavior: Misperceptions of Feedback in a Dynamic Decision Making Experiment", Management Science 35(3), 321-339 (exists: confirmed by econpapers listing in search). The summary seen: players act as managers of a beer-distribution inventory system; the estimated decision rule shows insensitivity to the supply line (feedback from own decisions) and most attribute the dynamics to external events although they are self-generated. Only a search-result summary was read; no paper text. Fu and Gonzalez (2006), Martin, Gonzalez and Lebiere (2004) and Schaffernicht challenge the strongest form (learning with practice, framing effects); not read.
- source: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:35:y:1989:i:3:p:321-339 (listing), plus a search summary. Publisher: INFORMS Management Science. pub 1989. confidence medium for existence, low for details (no full text). class: empirical-result. Peer-reviewed. Also an MIT working paper (dspace SWP-1933) exists.

F9. Validity/reliability evidence for a systems-thinking test: in this run I found NONE for the Bathtub Dynamics inventory (no Cronbach's alpha, no item-response data, no external validation in the conference text beyond rubric coding by the first author, coding criteria revised after testing; inter-rater reliability not seen in the portions I read). The authors present it as a tool under development. Replication evidence: the bathtub tasks "have been widely reused" with varied cover stories (Kapmeier et al. 2015 and others, from a search summary of SD Conference papers; not read) and reviewers say results support the original. confidence low-medium. class: validity-evidence (thin).
- Concept inventory with real psychometrics, but for adjacent construct: Soltis and McNeal, "Development and Validation of a Concept Inventory for Earth System Thinking Skills", Journal for STEM Education Research (2022): classical test theory plus IRT, about 1,004 MTurk respondents; "balancing" worded better than "negative" feedback for novices. Seen only through GSA abstracts and a Bohrium listing (grey aggregator). confidence low. class: validity-evidence. https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2021AM/webprogram/Paper364084.html

## Format cheat-sheet usable for test design (from F2, directly traced)
1. Given net-flow graph (piecewise-constant inflow/outflow): draw the stock. Test slope sign, turning points at net-flow zero crossings, quantity = area. Use both a bathtub and a cash/inventory cover story.
2. Sawtooth / changing inflow: slope of stock = net rate; curvature cases.
3. Step increase in demand with delay and a target stock: sketch production and inventory; test overshoot, 4-week lag, inventory dips first, and the conservation/area check (overshoot area = lost inventory).
4. Distinguish pattern recall from structure: ask for areas/quantities (e.g., "how many units must be added to restore inventory?"), not just "does it oscillate".
