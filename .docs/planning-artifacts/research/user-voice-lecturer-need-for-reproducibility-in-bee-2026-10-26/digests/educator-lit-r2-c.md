## Findings

1. Calgary authors run the MANUAL game for ~400 undergrads (about 100 simultaneous boards) with a fixed pre-printed demand-card deck; the same deck is used for every board ("initial demand already written on them"; "demand cards"; game ends by a "stop card" as the 37th customer demand card). Implication: identical demand across all teams is the default and the logistics reason (cards in a kit), not stated as a fairness requirement. | https://ucalgary.scholaris.ca/bitstreams/a2ba791f-01dc-4ab9-b30b-5150fc59379b/download (PDF; record https://ucalgary.scholaris.ca/handle/1880/51105) | Decision Sciences Institute (conference proceedings, DSI 41st Annual Meeting, pp. 3251-3258), authors Snider, Balakrishnan, da Silveira | 2010-11 | 2026-10-26 | medium (single source; "same deck for all boards" is inferred from kit description, not stated as a design rationale); STALE (>18 months) | experience-report

2. Results are compared across teams for debrief, not for grade: the cover page collects "their individual inventory costs for comparison purposes during the debrief session"; graphs are taped to the wall "so that their results can be easily compared"; instructor then shows "average results by position from the MIT students ... for benchmarking purposes" so students "put their perceived poor performance in context". | same URL | DSI proceedings | 2010-11 | 2026-10-26 | medium; STALE | experience-report

3. Grading is by ATTENDANCE only, deliberately not performance: "we provide participants with 2% of the course grade as an incentive to attend"; non-attenders "receive zero on this course component". Only 1.5% non-attendance reported. | same URL | DSI proceedings | 2010-11 | 2026-10-26 | medium; STALE | experience-report

4. They advise AGAINST prizes for the winning team in a large class: "Don't give prizes to the winning team ... in such a large group, this creates an incentive to ignore the game rules (e.g. not to verbally discuss order quantities) and to coordinate efforts to 'win', thereby subverting the intended learning experience." Relevance: performance ranking across teams was seen as harmful, which weakens the "comparable results for fair grading" need. | same URL | DSI proceedings | 2010-11 | 2026-10-26 | medium; STALE | guidance

5. Spoiler/memorisation concern exists but is about the web, not about replay: "Don't call it 'The Beer Game' prior to the game - some students search the Internet and in seconds get the 'secrets' of the beer game. To minimize this 'spoiler' risk, we simply refer to it as the 'Supply Chain Management Exercise'". | same URL | DSI proceedings | 2010-11 | 2026-10-26 | medium; STALE | experience-report

6. Debrief is delayed: "Don't debrief immediately after the game"; debrief in next lecture "typically within 48 hours". Teaching-effectiveness evidence: "I learned a lot in this course" item compared across 14 sections (508 students) before vs 13 sections (305 students) after game inclusion (ANOVA; result not read in detail). | same URL | DSI proceedings | 2010-11 | 2026-10-26 | medium; STALE | empirical (conference, not clearly peer-reviewed journal)

7. Online game study (Jeong & Hong): abstract states "students obtained significant knowledge and learning when the game was repeated; the communication level was a significant factor, whereas the demand pattern was not." Computer-based online BDSG; regression model; students experiments. NOT verified beyond the abstract: n, number of demand patterns, how communication level was operationalised. Full text paywalled (inderscienceonline 403). ## Looked for but not found
- ITE 10.1287/ited.2017.0174: not surfaced by any search; the title/topic is unconfirmed here. ited.2015.0138 resolved to Wright, "The Zu Zitter Game" (ITE 15(3), 240-253), a course-long operations game, NOT a beer game paper; not opened.
- No educator source (2020-2026) found stating that identical demand is required for fair grading, or complaining about unfair grading from different demand.
- No source found on students "remembering" a demand pattern from a first run when replaying the same scenario; only the web-spoiler concern (finding 5) and the Jeong & Hong repeat-play learning gain (finding 7).
- No source found on randomising demand per team for assessment, or on running two sections with different seeds and normalising results.
- MIT CTL "same or different for each team" question: not found (deck unreachable).
- Nothing from 2020-2026 educator-written material surfaced except vendor docs (Zensimu 2026); all peer-reviewed/proceedings hits are 1998-2011, i.e. stale for the 18-month freshness rule.
- Absence of evidence is itself a finding: public educator literature does not appear to treat cross-team demand identity as a grading-fairness issue; the one large-class report avoids performance-based grading altogether (finding 3, 4).
